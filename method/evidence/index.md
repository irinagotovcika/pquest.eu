    <h2>Evidence-informed by design</h2>

    <p>
      PQuest is designed as an evidence-informed psychological skills training
      method.
    </p>

    <p>
      Its development draws on psychological research relevant to learning,
      active practice, feedback, contextual variation, and the transfer of
      training. These principles inform the rationale behind the method and
      the way its Programme Theory is constructed.<sup><a href="#ref-3">[3]</a>–<a href="#ref-7">[7]</a></sup>
    </p>

    <p>
      Evidence for individual principles, however, is not the same as evidence
      for PQuest as a whole. Their integration within the PQuest Method
      represents a theoretically grounded approach whose specific effects
      require empirical investigation.
    </p>

    <p>
      For this reason, PQuest distinguishes between theoretical rationale,
      evidence supporting individual principles, and evidence about the effects
      of PQuest itself.
    </p>

    <h2>Evidence &amp; limitations</h2>

    <p>
      PQuest is an evidence-informed method, but evidence supporting its
      underlying principles should not be interpreted as evidence that the
      PQuest Method itself is effective.
    </p>

    <p>
      The specific effects of PQuest Trainings require empirical investigation.
      In particular, improvements observed within a training environment should
      not automatically be assumed to transfer to real-world behaviour or to
      broader psychological outcomes.<sup><a href="#ref-6">[6]</a>,<a href="#ref-7">[7]</a></sup>
    </p>

    <p>
      PQuest therefore treats learning within the training, real-world
      performance, and transfer as distinct questions rather than
      interchangeable measures of success.
    </p>

    <p>
      As research on PQuest develops, evidence relating specifically to the
      method and to individual PQuest Trainings will be reported separately
      from the literature supporting their theoretical foundations.
    </p>
